Guide for organizers

What you need to organize your first event: the real checklist

By SOMOS DER · July 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Almost every "organize your first event" guide starts with inspiration. This one starts with what you will be asked for: the numbers, the form fields and the decisions that blow up on you later if you don't make them first. It is the list we wish we'd had.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

1. The numbers, before the flyer

Add up everything you will have to pay no matter what: venue or rental, sound, artists, security, cleaning, permits, promotion. That total divided by the price you plan to charge tells you how many tickets you need to sell to not lose money. It is a number, not a feeling, and it is worth having it written down before you fall in love with the idea.

If that number is higher than the venue's capacity, no flyer will fix it: you have to cut costs or raise the price.

2. The data the form will ask for

Having it together before you sit down to load the event saves you half an afternoon:

  • The event title and type (party, show, theater, festival, etc.).
  • Date and time. If they are not settled yet, they can be left as to be confirmed.
  • Venue and city. The venue name also accepts to be confirmed.
  • Flyer or image. You upload it or paste a link. Load it before sharing the sales link: it is what shows when someone passes it on through WhatsApp.
  • Description. What it is, who it's for, what it includes. Write it for someone who doesn't know you.
  • Ticket types, each with a price and a cap.

There are two more options worth knowing from day one: you can set up a multi-day event, with its dates, and you can keep it hidden to test it and share it only with whoever you want before making it public.

3. Ticket types and caps

Start simple. For a first event, two or three types are more than enough: a general one, a cheaper early tier and, if it makes sense, a VIP that includes something specific (a table, access to another area, a bar). A VIP that includes nothing is just an expensive ticket.

The cap for each type is required and it is your only safety net: when it sells out, it sells out. Enter the venue's real capacity minus what you set aside for complimentary tickets, press and production.

If you want more detail on how to stagger prices, it is covered in the guide to pricing and tiers.

4. How payment works and who pays the fee

At ENTRÁ the service charge is paid by the buyer, added on top of your ticket price and shown at checkout before confirming. For you that means simple math: you receive the price you set.

The flip side, worth keeping in mind when setting the price: the number your audience sees is a bit higher than the one you entered. Put yourself in the buyer's shoes and look at the final total before deciding.

If the event is free, the reservation has no charge for anyone.

5. Complimentary tickets: count them before, not after

Complimentary tickets are the classic hole in a first event. They show up one at a time, always with a good reason, and by the day of the show you have thirty people taking up space who didn't pay.

Do it the other way around: decide the number before opening sales, subtract it from the cap, and issue them from the dashboard with the reason noted. Each complimentary ticket is issued with its own QR and email, just like a sold ticket, so at the door they are checked like all the rest.

6. The door: who and with what

Decide this beforehand, not on the day of the event:

  • Who scans. You need at least one person dedicated only to that.
  • With what. A phone with a camera and the account enabled for access control.
  • What happens when a ticket is rejected. Someone has to be able to sort it out on the side without stopping the line.

The detail of how it works is in the access control guide. What matters now is that the door is in your budget and on your list of people, not a surprise.

7. The review before opening sales

Ten minutes that save you a week of messages:

  • Open your event's public page and read it as if you weren't you.
  • Check date, time, venue and city. It is the most expensive mistake and the most common one.
  • Review the price and cap of each ticket type.
  • Buy a test ticket or make a reservation if the event is free, and check that the email with the QR arrives.
  • Paste the link in a chat and see what the preview looks like.
  • Decide your refund policy and state it in the description. If you don't write it beforehand, you will improvise it at the worst moment.

Only then press Lanzar Venta Oficial (launch official sale). You open sales yourself, whenever you want: there is no scheduled date that opens them automatically.

8. Event day

Everyone who bought gets a reminder email the day before with the event details. You take care of three things: that the person on the door arrives before the audience, that they have battery and data, and that they know who to call if something doesn't add up.

If after this first date the idea is for events to become your job, the order for the business to exist (covering the risk, choosing what you sell, pricing and landing the first client) is in how to start your events business from scratch, an article by hito.agency (in Spanish).

Frequently asked questions

What do I need to start selling tickets for my first event?

At a minimum: date, venue, a flyer, ticket types with their price and cap, and a description of what the event is. At ENTRÁ you also need the organizer dashboard enabled: the account is created automatically, but permission to sell is activated by the team manually, so it is worth requesting it a few days before you want to open sales.

Can I publish an event if I don't have the venue or date confirmed yet?

Yes. The form lets you leave the date, time or venue as to be confirmed, so you can set up the page and start promoting while you close those details. You can also keep the event hidden and share it only with whoever you want. What you shouldn't do is sell tickets with the date up in the air: if it changes later, you have to notify everyone who already bought.

How much does the platform charge me to sell tickets?

The service charge is paid by the buyer, added on top of the ticket price and visible at checkout before confirming. That means you receive the price you set, with no commission deducted. For free events, the reservation has no charge for anyone.

How many complimentary tickets should I give at an event?

The ones you decided on before opening sales, not the ones that keep coming up. Set the number, subtract it from the cap you put on sale and note the reason for each one. Complimentary tickets take up real space in the venue and don't pay for the venue, so if you don't count them in advance you end up selling less than you thought or letting in more people than fit.
