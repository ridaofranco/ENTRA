Guide for organizers

How to price your event tickets and set up tiers without losing money

By SOMOS DER · July 29, 2026 · 6 min read

Almost nobody actually calculates a ticket price: they copy the event next door and add a little. Then they sell 60% of the venue at early bird prices and the numbers do not work. The price comes from your costs and from when you need the money, not from the market. The market comes later, to adjust.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to any ticketing platform; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

1. Your floor first, then the price

Before you write down a number, add up everything the event costs you whether it sells or not: venue, sound, lights, artists, security, insurance, production, cleaning, music rights (in Argentina, SADAIC), the people working the door.

Divide that total by the number of tickets you really think you will sell, not by the venue capacity. That is your break-even per ticket. It is an uncomfortable number, which is why almost nobody works it out: if it comes out higher than what you wanted to charge, the problem is not the price, it is the cost structure or the venue you picked.

Only with that floor in front of you do you look at what others charge. If your floor is well above the market you have three options: cut costs, pick a smaller venue or give people a reason to pay more. Lowering the price and hoping is not a fourth option.

2. The price you set is not the price your audience sees

This surprises a lot of people and it decides how you round. At ENTRÁ the organizer receives 100% of the price they set: if you price the ticket at ARS 10,000, you receive ARS 10,000. The service charge (ENTRÁ's 8% plus VAT, plus the payment processing cost) is paid by the buyer on top of your price, which puts the final total roughly 15% above it.

Before publishing, do this: open your event's public page on a phone and look at the big number, the final total. That is what your audience will really see, with the subtotal and the charge itemized. If you want the total to be a round number, adjust your price until it is; do not leave it to chance.

3. A tier closes when it sells out, not on a date

A "tier" is not a date on a flyer: it is a limited number of tickets at one price. When it sells out, it is gone, and that is the whole trick. The scarcity that makes people buy today is the quantity, not the calendar, because calendars always get stretched.

At ENTRÁ each tier is a ticket type, and each type has three fields: Tipo de Entrada (the name), Precio (price) and Cupo (Disponibles) (quantity available). The ones you mark as Early Bird are sold during the presale; when you press Lanzar Venta Oficial (launch official sale), the presale closes and the other types go on sale. Two honest warnings: you decide when to switch, there is no automatic change by date, and that button cannot be undone from the dashboard. Press it when you are sure.

When a type runs out, your audience sees AGOTADO (sold out), and when fewer than ten are left they see a last tickets notice. That works in your favor: showing that something is running out sells more than any flyer.

4. How many tiers, and how big a jump

For most events three is enough:

  • Early bird: small quantity, between 15% and 25% of capacity. It is not "the cheap one": it is the one that pays your suppliers' deposits.
  • General: the bulk. This is your real price, the one from step 1.
  • VIP or premium: small quantity and a clearly higher price. You do not add it to sell a lot of them, you add it so the general ticket looks reasonable next to it.

Between tiers, a jump of 15% to 25% is noticeable without scaring people off. Less than that creates no urgency; much more makes latecomers feel punished, and they do not buy. And one rule that is not up for discussion: the price never goes back down. If you reopen the early bird because sales are slow, you teach your audience to wait, and next time nobody buys early.

5. VIP has to include something concrete

"VIP" cannot be the same thing for more money: it has to give something you can name in one line. Early entry, a separate area with a view, a bar without a queue, a different entrance.

A practical detail: a ticket type is a name, a price and a quantity, so what the VIP includes does not explain itself. Put it in the event description and in your posts, using the same words as the ticket type name.

6. To sell cheaper, use codes

Lowering the published price is the fastest way to annoy everyone who already bought. If you need a special price for a group, a radio station or a partner, use a discount code: percentage or fixed amount, with a usage limit and an expiry date, and then check in the dashboard how many times each one was used. That gives you control over how much money you give up, and tells you where each sale came from.

7. Count complimentary tickets before, not after

Comps are the classic hole in a budget: press, artists, sponsors, the friend of whoever lent the venue. At ENTRÁ they are issued as tickets with their own QR and they do not reduce the stock for sale, but they do count toward capacity. In plain terms: if you have room for 500 and give away 80, you have 420 left to sell, and the break-even from step 1 has to be recalculated with 420.

Mistakes that cost money

  • Working out break-even on venue capacity instead of realistic sales.
  • Running an early bird with a big quantity: you sell half the room at the lowest price.
  • Reopening a tier that already closed.
  • Publishing without checking the final total on a phone.
  • Forgetting about comps until the week of the event.

Frequently asked questions

How many ticket tiers should I run?

For most events, three: an early bird with a small quantity, the general ticket and a premium or VIP type. Fewer than two leaves you without the urgency you need at the start; more than four is confusing and makes people wait to see if a cheaper one appears. What matters is not the number but that each tier has a set quantity and the price never goes back down.

How do I work out the minimum ticket price so I do not lose money?

Add up all the fixed costs of the event (venue, sound, artists, security, insurance, production) and divide them by the number of tickets you really expect to sell, not by venue capacity. That is your break-even per ticket: below it you lose money. Only then add your margin and look at what the competition charges, in that order.

If I price a ticket at ARS 10,000 on ENTRÁ, how much do I get and how much does the buyer pay?

You get the full ARS 10,000: the organizer receives 100% of the price they set. The buyer pays your price plus a service charge that includes ENTRÁ's 8% plus VAT and the payment processing cost, which puts the final total roughly 15% above your price. That total is shown on the event page and at checkout before the purchase is confirmed.

Can I change a ticket price after the event is published?

Yes. In the event dashboard, under the Tickets y Capacidad tab, you can edit the price and quantity of each ticket type at any time. But use it to move up a tier, not down: lowering the published price punishes everyone who already bought and teaches them to wait. If you want to sell cheaper to a specific group, use a discount code instead of touching the price.
