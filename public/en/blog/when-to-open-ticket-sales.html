Guide for organizers

When to open ticket sales for your event

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 5 min read

Open sales too early and you get weeks of silence; open them too late and you have no money to pay deposits and no time to correct course. There is no magic date, but there are questions that define it and signals in the first weeks that tell you whether you are on track. This guide puts them in order.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

What decides how early to open

No single number works for everyone. Four questions call the shots:

  • How much does your audience need to plan? If coming means traveling, taking the day off or finding someone to watch the kids, they need to know well in advance. If they live ten blocks away, they don't.
  • How much money do you need before the event? If you have to pay deposits to the venue or suppliers, advance sales are part of how you finance the event, and opening late leaves you without that margin.
  • How much do people want to see it? A band with a loyal following or a party with a name can open early and sell. A new idea nobody knows yet needs content and promotion before sales start moving.
  • How much material do you have to communicate? Every week of open sales needs something to say. Opening far in advance and then going quiet cools things down more than it helps.

Rough guides by event type

Take these as a starting point, not a rule, and adjust them with what you see in your own audience:

  • A party or a show in a bar. People decide close to the date. Opening a few weeks ahead is usually enough, with a short presale at the start that rewards whoever commits first.
  • A gig with a known band or a special date. Better to open earlier: fans want to secure their spot, and the first hours of sales tell you a lot.
  • A festival or multi-day event. This is the one that needs the most lead time, because people plan travel, lodging and days off around it.
  • A play with a run of shows. Open the first performances before opening night and add dates as sales move, instead of publishing the whole season at once.
  • A workshop or class with limited spots. Just enough for people to put it in their calendar and for you to decide in time whether it goes ahead, based on sign-ups.

At ENTRÁ, every event goes through a review before it is published, usually the same day. Keep that in mind if you want to open at an announced time: load it with some margin.

Open with a reason to buy now

Sales don't start because you open them; they start because someone has a reason to buy that day. The most common ones:

  • A cheaper presale with a date or quantity limit, stated clearly: until when it lasts or how many there are.
  • An announcement with something new: the lineup, the venue, a guest.
  • A heads-up to your audience ("sales open Thursday at noon"), so the first day is not silence.

How to read the first weeks

It is common for sales to have two moments: a peak when you open and another close to the date, with a plateau in between. Sales slowing down after day one is normal; what matters is what you do with that plateau. What to look at:

  • How much you sold in the first peak compared with your break-even point. Not against a full house, against what you need so you don't lose money.
  • Which ticket type is moving. If the presale sells out right away, maybe you priced it too low or released too few. If nobody buys it, the reason to buy now is not working.
  • What day and time purchases come in. It tells you when to post.
  • Which channel brings people. A different discount code per channel or per promoter gives you a clue: the dashboard shows how many times each one was used.

Signals to correct course in time

  • A long plateau with no announcements: it is not the price, it is silence. Schedule something to say every week.
  • Lots of visits and few purchases: check the event page. If it is not clear what it is, when it is and what is included, people leave without buying.
  • It sold out sooner than you expected: next time, open later or with a smaller presale.
  • A few days to go and you are far from break-even: decide in time whether you cut costs, add promotion or move the date. The worst decision is the one made the night before.

Frequently asked questions

How far ahead should I open ticket sales?

It depends on how much your audience needs to plan, how much money you need before the event and how much you have to say while sales are open. A neighborhood party can open a few weeks ahead; a festival people travel to needs much more lead time.

Is it normal for sales to slow down after the first day?

Yes, it happens often: a peak when you open, another close to the date and a plateau in between. What moves it is having something new to announce and a reason to buy earlier, like a tier that is about to close.

What do I look at to know if sales are going well?

Compare what you sold with your break-even point, not with a full house. Also look at which ticket type is moving, on which days and times purchases come in and which channel brings people.
