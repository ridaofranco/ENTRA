A show with several bands: how to split the takings

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

A night shared by three or four bands, or a small afternoon festival, has a problem that doesn't come up when only one band plays: at the end of the night the money has to be split, and every band thinks it brought more people than the others. The way to avoid the argument is to agree on the split before opening sales and measure who sold what from day one. This guide goes step by step.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

First, the agreement in writing

Before publishing the event, get the bands together (or whoever represents them) and put in writing, even if it is just a message in the group chat:

  • What gets deducted before splitting: venue, sound, technician, paid promotion, whatever the promoter charges.
  • How what's left is split: equal shares, by sales or with a guarantee (all three are below).
  • What happens with door sales and with each band's guest tickets.
  • When people get paid: that same night or when the ticketing platform pays out the money.
  • The running order and each band's set length, because playing at 9 pm or at 1 am changes how many people each one brings.

How many tickets to put on sale

The ceiling is the venue's authorized capacity; never sell more than that. For the details, read authorized capacity: how many tickets to sell. But to do the numbers, don't start from a full venue: ask each band how many people they brought to their last show and add it up. If each one brings forty, the night has a hundred and sixty, not the three hundred that fit.

Divide the costs by that number and you will know whether the price works. If the math only works with a full venue, the price is too low or the night has too many expenses. For the full calculation, see how many tickets to sell so you don't lose money.

With ENTRÁ you receive 100% of the price you set for each ticket. The service and processing fees are paid by the buyer, with the breakdown visible before paying. That simplifies the split: the takings are the price times the number sold.

Three ways to split

Equal shares

Expenses are deducted and what's left is divided by the number of bands. It is the simplest option and causes the least arguing when the bands draw similar crowds. The problem appears when one brings twice as many as the rest: in the long run it stops wanting to share a bill.

By each band's sales

Each band gets paid according to the tickets it sold. It is the fairest option when the crowds are very different, but it only works if you can measure well who sold what (how is further down). It has a good side effect: every band promotes, because every ticket adds to their share.

Guarantee

One band, usually the headliner, is guaranteed a fixed amount no matter what, and the rest is split among the others using one of the other two methods. It helps you land a band that draws a crowd, but whoever gives the guarantee takes on the risk: if the night sells poorly, that money still goes out.

You can also mix them: one part equal for everyone (for example, half of what's left) and the other according to sales. That way no band leaves empty handed and the one that sells most earns most.

How to know how much each band sold

If the split depends on sales, you need a figure nobody can dispute. ENTRÁ has two ways to get it:

  • One link per band. Each band shares the event link with a different parameter, for example ?utm_source=instagram&utm_campaign=band-1. ENTRÁ stores where each purchase came from: the event dashboard shows it in the where sales came from block, and the sales export includes the source and campaign columns. With that you add up purchases per band without lowering the price.
  • One discount code per band. "BAND1", "BAND2", each with its own cap and expiry date. In the list of codes, each one shows how many times it was used. It gives each band something to offer its fans, in exchange for selling a little cheaper.

Two details so you don't get surprised:

  • Both methods count purchases, not tickets. If someone buys four tickets with a band's code, that is one use. For the split, look at the quantity column in the sales export, which shows how many tickets each purchase has.
  • Not everything can be attributed. Someone who goes straight to the event from a search engine, buys at the door or arrives without a link isn't assigned to any band. Agree beforehand that this part goes into a common pool.

How codes and utm links work is explained in detail in discount codes for an event and in how to promote an event and track sales.

Guest tickets and the door

  • Guest tickets: agree on how many each band gets. Comps are issued separately from the dashboard and show up in the attendee list as "Cortesía" (comp), so they don't mix with what was sold. More in complimentary tickets: how many to give.
  • Door: anyone who arrives without a ticket can buy from their phone on the event page while sales are still open. At the door the QR code is scanned and each one is valid only once.
  • At the end: export the sales, add them up per band and show them the file. With the numbers in plain sight, the split takes five minutes.

Frequently asked questions

How do I know how many tickets each band sold?

There are two ways in ENTRÁ. One link per band with a different utm_campaign: the dashboard groups sales by source and campaign, and the sales export includes those columns. Or one discount code per band: each code shows how many times it was used. The link measures without lowering the price; the code also gives the band something to offer.

Is it better to split equally or by what each band sells?

Equal shares is simplest when the bands draw similar crowds. By sales is fairer when one brings far more people than another, but it requires measuring well who sold what. Many shows use a mix: one part equal for everyone and another according to each band's sales.

What happens with tickets sold at the door?

Door sales have no band link or code, so they can't be attributed. The usual approach is to agree beforehand that this money goes into a common pool split in equal shares, or in the same proportion as advance sales.
