Guide for organizers

Reserved seating or general admission: which one suits you

By SOMOS DER · October 30, 2026 · 6 min read

Before setting a price, you have to decide how people will be placed: each one in their own seat or wherever they find room. It looks like a detail, but it changes how you sell, how long the door takes and what arguments you will have that night. This guide explains when each option works, what ENTRÁ does and does not do, and how to set up sections with different prices without numbering anything.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

First, what ENTRÁ does and doesn't do

So there are no surprises: ENTRÁ has no seating map and no numbered seats. Buyers don't pick "row F, seat 12" on a plan. What it does have is ticket types, which in the dashboard are also called sections, and each one has its own name, price and capacity. When a type sells out, it shows as sold out and nobody else can choose it, while the others stay on sale.

That covers the vast majority of small events: one general section, or several sections with different prices and open seating within each one. If your venue requires an assigned seat for every person, it is worth knowing before choosing a ticketing platform. Further down is how to handle the in between cases.

What changes between one and the other

  • General admission. The ticket gets you in, and inside everyone stands or sits wherever they want or can. It is the format for parties, gigs in bars, folk music nights, fairs and almost all talks. It sells fast, the door only checks that the QR code is valid and there is no sorting out who sat in whose seat.
  • Reserved seating. Each ticket has a fixed location. It is the format for large theaters with fixed seats, stadiums with stands and some gala dinners. It gives the buyer peace of mind (they don't have to arrive early for a good spot), but it requires an exact plan of the room and someone to seat people.

In the middle is what small organizers use most: general admission sections. The room is divided into zones with different prices (front and back, tables and bar, stalls and balcony) and within each zone seating is first come, first served.

When general admission is enough

If you recognize yourself in any of these cases, general admission or general sections are more than enough:

  • People are standing for most of the event: party, gig, electronic music party, after office.
  • The chairs are loose and get arranged depending on how many people come. In a multipurpose room, a cultural center or a bar, numbering chairs that move makes no sense.
  • The audience is a few hundred and knows each other: a dance recital, a music series, an independent theater show.
  • Sales are last minute. If half buy in the final hours or at the door, general admission avoids someone paying and then finding out only scattered spots at the back are left.

For these events, what matters most is the total number: how many people get in without going over what the venue allows. That calculation is in how many tickets to sell based on the venue's authorized capacity.

How to set up sections in ENTRÁ

When you create the event you load the ticket types, and from the event dashboard you can edit prices and quantities or add new sections at any time. A neighborhood theater with two zones would look like this:

  • Stalls · rows 1 to 8, with its price and a capacity equal to the seats in those rows.
  • Stalls · rows 9 to 16, with a lower price and its capacity.
  • Balcony, if the room has one, with its price and capacity.

And a show in a bar with tables:

  • Front table, with a capacity equal to the chairs at those tables.
  • Back table, cheaper.
  • Bar or standing, the cheapest, with the capacity of the space left over.

Some details that help:

  • The name says where the person will be. It is what the buyer sees and what the door sees when scanning. "Rows 1 to 8" is clear; "Gold Section" needs explaining.
  • Each section's capacity is the real number of spots in that zone. Each type has its own capacity and they are not shared. If you add them all up, the total has to match what fits in the room, comps included.
  • Invitations have their place. If you give comps, load them from the dashboard with a name and reason, so they have their own QR code and don't suddenly show up taking the front row. How many to give is in complimentary tickets: how many to give.
  • If you want tiers, they combine with sections: you can mark types as presale and launch official sales from the dashboard whenever you want. How to stagger prices is in how to price tickets and set up tiers.

Numbered tables: the in between case

There are events where each group wants to know which table is theirs: a dinner show, a graduation party, a bingo night at a club. If there are only a few tables, you can handle it with ticket types and no map:

  • One type per table, with the number in the name and the capacity of that table's chairs: "Table 1 · 4 seats", "Table 2 · 4 seats". When all four are sold, that table shows as sold out.
  • A sketch in the event description or in the image, with the table numbers, so buyers know where table 1 is and where table 12 is.
  • At the door, the scanner shows the ticket type of each QR code, so the person receiving guests knows which table to send each one to.

This works with ten or fifteen tables. With forty it is already a long list for the buyer, and it is better to group them ("Tables of 4 · front", "Tables of 4 · back") and assign the number at the door, in order of arrival or with a list you put together yourself from the dashboard's buyer export.

What to say on the event page

Since there are no assigned spots within each section, the rule has to be clear before buying:

  • "Seating is first come, first served within each section." Said plainly, close to the price.
  • What time the room opens, so people who want to be at the front know when to arrive.
  • Whether people can save seats for someone arriving later. Decide it beforehand and write it down; it is the most common argument in an unnumbered room.
  • What each section includes: whether "Front table" comes with a drink, whether the bar has stools or is standing.

The door, depending on how you sold

With general admission or sections, the door is simple:

  • The QR code is scanned from a phone. Each QR is valid only once: if someone sent a screenshot to someone else, whoever arrives first gets in.
  • The scanner shows the ticket type, so if you have physically separate sections (stalls and a balcony with its own staircase), the person at the door tells each one which way to go.
  • If someone can't find the email, they can be looked up by surname, ID number (DNI) or email from the scanner.
  • If you have an usher, make sure they know where each section ends. A rope, a tape or a sign at row 9 keeps the cheapest ticket from sitting at the front.

When you do need reserved seating

To be honest: if your event is in a large room with fixed seats, hundreds of places, and the audience expects to pick their seat on a plan, you need a ticketing platform with a seating map. Same if the venue requires by contract that you sell with assigned seats. For everything else, which is nearly everything a small organizer does, general sections give you different prices, clear capacities and a door that doesn't jam.

Frequently asked questions

Does ENTRÁ sell tickets with numbered seats?

No. ENTRÁ has no seating map and no numbered seats. What it does have is ticket types with their own name, price and capacity, which you can use to set up sections: stalls, balcony, front tables, general. Within each section, seating is first come, first served.

When is general admission the better choice?

At most small events: parties, gigs in bars, folk music nights, fairs, talks, rooms with loose chairs. It is simpler to sell and to control, and the door only has to validate the QR code. If you want different prices by distance from the stage, set up general sections with their own capacity.

What if I still want each person to have their own spot?

If there are only a few locations, like tables, you can sell one ticket type per table with that table's capacity, for example "Table 1 · 4 seats". If it is a large room with hundreds of seats that need to be assigned, you need a ticketing platform with a seating map.
