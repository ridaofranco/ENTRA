Guide for organizers

Complimentary tickets and guest lists: how many to give and how to control them

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

Complimentary tickets are the quietest leak an event has. Nobody writes them down as a cost, but every one of them is a spot you did not sell. Used well, they bring you press, happy artists and people who drive sales; used badly, they fill the venue with people who did not pay and leave you not knowing how much you really sold.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

A comp is not a free ticket: it is a ticket you don't charge for

It sounds like the same thing and it isn't. Anyone can grab a free ticket; a comp is something you give, to a named person, for a reason. And it takes up a spot exactly like a sold one: the person gets in, buys drinks, uses the bathroom and counts toward the venue capacity. That is why comps have to be decided in advance, not handed out as people ask for them.

If your event is free for everyone, you are not talking about comps but about reservations with a cap. That is a different setup.

Who should get them and who shouldn't

The comps that make sense are the ones that give you something back or that the event needs in order to work:

  • Artists and their crew. Whatever the agreement with each one says, in writing.
  • Press and people who spread the word. If they are going to talk about it, give them a spot.
  • Sponsors and the venue. These often come as part of the deal.
  • People who help you sell. Promoters and anyone who moves tickets.

The ones worth stopping are the last minute "put me on the list" requests. Not because those people don't deserve to go, but because each one is a spot you take away from someone who was going to pay, and at a small event that difference shows at the till.

How many: set a number before sales open

There is no right figure for every event, and be wary of anyone who gives you a magic percentage. What does work is setting a written cap before you open sales, based on your own numbers: how many tickets you need to sell to cover costs and how many you have left over within the capacity. Comps come out of that margin, never out of the spots you need to sell. If you haven't worked out that number yet, start with how to price your tickets.

Split the cap by group (artists, press, sponsors, crew) and give each group someone in charge. When somebody asks you for one, you know which pool it comes from and whether there is any left.

How to issue them so things don't get out of hand

A list on paper or in a phone note has three problems: you don't know who used it, you don't know if someone passed it on, and it causes arguments at the door. The tidy way is for each comp to be one more ticket, with its own QR code, issued in a person's name.

In ENTRÁ, comps are generated from the event dashboard, in their own tab. For each one you enter:

  • Name and email of the person receiving it (required), plus phone and ID number (DNI) if you want.
  • The ticket type you are giving and the quantity.
  • The reason, so you know later why each one was given.

Each comp comes with its own unique QR code and reaches the person by email, just like a purchased ticket. If you entered the phone number, you can also send it by WhatsApp from the same dashboard. And since it is one more ticket, it is scanned at the door the same way and is valid only once: no list to search and no names to spell out.

After the event: check how many were used

This is what almost nobody does and what teaches you the most. In the dashboard, the attendee list can be filtered between purchased and comps, and in the export each ticket shows its origin: "Compra" (purchase) or "Cortesía" (comp). With that you can answer concrete questions:

  • How many of the comps you gave were actually used?
  • Which group asked for the most and showed up the least?
  • How many people got in overall, counting both?

If a group asks for a lot and shows up little, give them fewer at the next event. If the press ones are all used, maybe it is worth adding a few. Same with sales: if you want to know where purchases came from, see what data a ticketing platform should give you.

What to avoid

  • Giving comps without a name. A QR code floating around a WhatsApp group gets used by whoever arrives first.
  • Mixing comps with sales. If you don't keep them apart, you don't know how much you really sold.
  • Handing them out on the day. Anything issued at the last minute is harder to control at the door.
  • Using comps to cover weak sales. Filling the venue with free tickets can work once; if it becomes a habit, your audience learns to wait for the invitation.

Frequently asked questions

What is a complimentary ticket at an event?

It is a ticket the organizer gives without charging to a specific person and for a reason: artists, press, sponsors or someone who helps sell. It takes up a spot just like a sold ticket, so it is worth deciding how many to give before opening sales, not as people ask for them.

How many guest tickets should I give for an event?

There is no percentage that works for everyone. The useful thing is to set a written cap before opening sales, based on your break even point: comps come out of the margin you have left within the capacity, never out of the tickets you need to sell to cover costs.

How are comps generated in ENTRÁ?

From the event dashboard, in the comps tab. You enter the name and email of the person receiving it, optionally phone and ID number (DNI), the ticket type, the quantity and the reason. Each comp comes with its own QR code, arrives by email and, if you entered the phone, can also be sent by WhatsApp.

Can I find out how many comps were used?

Yes. In the dashboard, the attendee list can be filtered between purchased tickets and comps, and in the export each ticket shows its origin. That way you see how many of the ones you gave were used and which group asked for more than actually came.
