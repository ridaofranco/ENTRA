Charity or benefit event: how to sell tickets transparently

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

A benefit event has something an ordinary party doesn't: people pay for the ticket because they trust the money gets where you said it would. That trust is earned before selling and confirmed afterwards, when you share how much was raised. This guide goes in order: what to tell people, how to set the price, what to do with complimentary tickets and how to report back without leaving any doubt.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

Before selling: decide and write down where the money goes

Most problems at a charity event show up because nobody made it clear from the start what part of the ticket goes to the cause. Settle it before publishing:

  • Who receives it? A person, a family, a community kitchen, a school parents' association, a club. By name and, if possible, with someone on their side who knows about the event and shares it.
  • What part of each ticket gets there? There are two honest ways: "everything raised is donated" (and someone else covers the costs) or "what is left after paying the costs is donated". Both are fine. What is not fine is saying the first and doing the second.
  • What costs are there? Venue, sound, security, flyers, drinks if there is a bar. Write them down beforehand, with what you already know you will pay, so you don't discover expenses the day after.

Whatever you decide goes in one or two sentences in the event description and on the flyer. Something like "What we raise, minus the venue and sound costs, goes to the neighborhood community kitchen. We will post the total on Monday." Short, concrete and with a date.

The price: a ticket, a solidarity ticket or free entry

The price has to be easy to pay for the audience you are bringing in. Three ways to set it up:

  • A single price. The simplest to communicate and to check at the door.
  • Two ticket types. A general one and a "solidarity ticket" at a higher price for anyone who wants to give more. Each ticket type has its own price and cap, and the dashboard shows how many of each were sold.
  • Free with reservation. If you prefer everyone to get in and raise money with a collection, a raffle or the bar, you can set up free tickets with a reservation and QR so you know how many people are coming.

If the event is a party or a show, the usual still applies: opening with a cheaper presale helps people commit early.

With ENTRÁ you receive 100% of the price you set for each ticket. The service and processing charge is paid by the buyer, who sees it itemized at checkout before paying. For a charity's accounts that is an advantage: the price you announce is what comes in for each ticket sold, with no commission deductions to explain later.

Complimentary tickets: few, with a reason and in the open

At a charity event each invitation is a ticket that adds nothing to the cause, so it pays to handle them more carefully than at an ordinary event:

  • Set the number before selling. Artists who play for free, the venue people who lend the room, whoever receives the donation, someone from the press. Beyond that, everyone pays.
  • Write down the reason for each one. In ENTRÁ each complimentary ticket is loaded with the person's details and a reason, and it reaches them with its own QR. When you close the accounts you will be able to say how many there were and why.

Complimentary tickets don't come out of the sales cap but they do count toward capacity, so the dashboard shows you the real total of people who will get in. How many to give without draining what you raise is in complimentary tickets and invitations: how many to give.

During sales: one channel and one number

When lots of people help sell, the count gets lost. Three rules that keep it tidy:

  • One sales link for everyone. Nobody should collect cash in hand or by transfer to their personal account. If you want to sell at the door, keep online sales open during the event and have people buy from their phone in the line, with the same link.
  • Watch sales in real time. The dashboard shows who bought, when and which ticket type. Share progress with the group so everyone knows how far there is to go.
  • Work out how many you need to sell to cover costs. Up to that number, the money pays for the event. From there on, it goes to the cause. How to do that calculation is in how many tickets to sell so you don't lose money.

At the door

On the day of the event, entry is checked like any other: each ticket has a unique QR that works only once and is scanned from a phone. If you have a collection, raffle or bar, keep that money separate from ticket money from the start, with its own person in charge and its own count at the end of the night.

Afterwards: reporting back

This is the part most often forgotten and the one that weighs most for the next event. A good report has four lines:

  • What was sold: number of tickets by type and total amount. It comes from the dashboard.
  • What was deducted: each cost with its amount, if you said you would deduct costs.
  • What was raised separately: collection, raffle, bar, each with its number.
  • What was handed over: the final amount and proof (a transfer, a photo of the handover, a message from whoever received it).

Post it on the date you promised, in the same place where you promoted the event. From the dashboard you can also export the buyer list to tell the people who came how the accounts ended up and to let them know about the next event (look after it: it is personal data).

The tax side of a benefit event (whether you need to issue invoices, how to declare what is collected and what is donated) depends on who is organizing: an individual, a nonprofit association or a school parents' association are not in the same situation. This guide is not tax advice: ask your accountant before opening sales.

Frequently asked questions

What do I need to tell people at a benefit event?

Who receives the money, what part of each ticket gets to them (all of it or what is left after costs) and when you will share how much was raised. Put it in the event description and on the flyer, in one or two clear sentences.

Can I run a charity event with free entry and reservations?

Yes. If you prefer everyone to get in and raise money with a collection, a raffle or a bar, you can set up free tickets with a reservation and QR. That way you know how many people are coming even if you don't charge for entry.

How do I report back after a benefit event?

Post a summary with what was sold, the costs that were deducted, the amount handed over and proof of the handover. The ENTRÁ dashboard gives you the total sold by ticket type, which is the starting point for that summary.

Does a benefit event have to issue invoices or pay taxes?

It depends on who is organizing (an individual, a nonprofit association, a school parents' association) and how the money is collected. This guide is not tax advice: ask your accountant before opening sales.
