Guide for organizers

Ticketed fashion show: front row, press and capacity

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

A clothing brand presenting a collection has two audiences in the same room: people who pay to see the show and people the brand needs inside no matter what, like press, influencers, buyers and friends. If you mix everything in a WhatsApp list, on the night of the show the door doesn't know who is who. This guide sorts out sales, invitations, capacity and schedule so the runway is the only thing that has to go perfectly.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

What you are selling

Before loading anything, define what the event is. A twenty minute show with a runway and chairs is not the same as a collection launch with a DJ, drinks and racks to look at up close. The questions you need to be able to answer:

  • How long the main part lasts: the walk itself is usually short, and what surrounds it (reception, toast, garment sales) is what fills the night.
  • Whether there are chairs or people stand: with chairs, capacity is set by the number of chairs; standing, it is set by the venue and what it is authorized for.
  • Whether the ticket includes something: a drink, a discount on the collection, a bag with products. If it does, it goes in the name of the ticket type.
  • Who gets in free: press, influencers, store buyers, the brand's team. You need that list before opening sales, not the week of the show.

General admission and front row

The clearest way to sell a fashion show is with two ticket types:

  • General admission: standing or in the back rows. This is the one that sells volume.
  • Front row: more expensive, with a capacity equal to the chairs that fit right against the runway. If it includes something extra (a welcome drink, the brand's bag), say so in the name: "Front row + bag".

In ENTRÁ each ticket type has its name, price and capacity, and it closes on its own when it fills up. What ENTRÁ does not have is numbered seating: there is no room map and nobody picks seat 4 in row A. The front row works as a section, not as numbered seats. In practice you handle it like this:

  • Count the real chairs, with the runway set up, and use that number as the capacity of the "Front row" type. Not one more.
  • Hold the front row chairs for guests (press, an influencer or two). Those chairs are not sold: if 30 fit in the row and 8 are for guests, the "Front row" type goes on sale with a capacity of 22.
  • Put someone from the team in the row to seat people as they come in. The scanner shows the door the ticket type of each QR code, so whoever has "Front row" goes straight to the front.
  • If you want to mark chairs with the names of outlets or guests, do it with printed signs. That stays outside the ticketing platform and doesn't need to be on the ticket.

Each purchase allows up to 4 tickets of each type. For a fashion show that is fine: the front row isn't bought by a group of ten, and if someone wants more, they make another purchase. To decide how many to put on sale in total, see authorized capacity: how many tickets to sell.

Invitations for press and influencers

Invitations are the most delicate part of a fashion show, because these are the people who will later show the collection. If they arrive and aren't listed anywhere, that is your problem. With ENTRÁ you issue them as comps from the event dashboard:

  • You enter the guest's details: name, email, WhatsApp or phone and ID number (DNI).
  • You choose the ticket type (general or front row) and the quantity. If an outlet comes with a photographer, that's two.
  • The guest gets their QR code by email, just like someone who bought. From the list of issued comps you can resend it, download it or send it by WhatsApp.
  • At the door it is scanned like any ticket. Each QR is valid only once, so one invitation can't be shared among five people.

Comps don't come out of the capacity you have on sale, but they do count toward the venue's capacity. Do the math backwards: total capacity, minus invitations, equals what you can sell. Some guidelines that save trouble:

  • Send invitations early, one or two weeks before, with a clear arrival time. The press plans its schedule ahead.
  • Ask for confirmation. If someone doesn't come, the front row chair sits empty in every photo. A short list of replacements saves the picture.
  • Keep the brand's guests and the venue's guests separate. If the venue asks for spots for its people, issue those as comps too, so everything stays in the same check-in.

How many to give and to whom is covered in more detail in complimentary tickets: how many to give.

Capacity and price

A small fashion show doesn't pay for itself with tickets alone: there are usually brands supporting it, garment sales that night or a launch budget. Still, it is worth having the ticket cover a real share of the cost. Add up venue, sound, lighting, models, hair and makeup, photography and promotion, and compare it against the number of tickets you think you will sell, not against a full room.

  • The front row has to be worth the difference. If it costs double, it should include something noticeable: the best spot and something from the brand.
  • General admission can have tiers: a cheaper presale for the brand's followers and a final price in the last week.
  • A discount code for the brand's customers works well: you send it by email or Instagram and measure how many came that way.

With ENTRÁ you receive 100% of the price you set for each ticket. The service and processing fees are paid by the buyer, with the breakdown visible before paying.

Schedule

The show starts at a specific time and people arrive earlier. On the event page, write both times: when the doors open and when the first model walks out. If the show starts at 8 pm and people are still coming in at 8:10, the runway gets interrupted and the front row gets seated with the collection already moving.

  • Open the doors at least 45 minutes before, and ask the press to arrive in the first half hour.
  • Enter the end time in the event. If you don't, ENTRÁ closes online sales 3 hours after the start. For a show with a party afterwards, entering the real end time keeps sales open as long as it makes sense.
  • Decide what happens with latecomers: whether they come in during the walk or wait until the end. Write it on the page.

The door

  • Two lines if you can: one for guests and press, another for the public. The scanner is the same, but the press doesn't wait behind forty people.
  • The QR code is scanned from a phone. If someone can't find the email, they can be looked up by surname, ID number or email from the scanner.
  • The scanner shows the type: "Front row" goes to the front with the person seating people, "General" goes to the back.

For the rest of the night at the door, see access control on the day of the event.

Frequently asked questions

Can I sell numbered seats for the front row?

ENTRÁ does not have numbered seating. The front row is sold as a separate ticket type, with its own price and a capacity equal to the chairs that fit at the front. Inside, someone from the team seats those people in the row in order of arrival.

How do I invite the press and influencers?

With comps from the event dashboard. You enter the guest's name, email, phone and ID number (DNI), choose the ticket type and quantity, and they get their own QR code by email. You can also resend it or send it by WhatsApp from the list of issued comps.

Do invitations take spots away from sales?

They don't come out of the capacity you have on sale, but they do count toward the venue's capacity. If the room holds 150 people and you are giving out 30 invitations, put 120 on sale across all ticket types.
