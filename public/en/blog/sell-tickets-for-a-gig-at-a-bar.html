Guide for organizers

Selling tickets to play at a bar or cultural center

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

Playing at a bar or a cultural center is the most common format for a band that is starting out, and also the one that gets most complicated by things that have nothing to do with music: who collects the money, how many people fit, what happens with the bar and who stands at the door. This guide sorts all of that out before the first flyer goes out.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

First, the deal with the venue

Before setting a price you need to know how the money is split. At bars and cultural centers there are three usual deals, sometimes combined:

  • The door goes to the band. The venue earns from the bar and the band keeps what comes in from tickets. It works if the bar sells plenty of drinks and the band brings people.
  • A percentage of the door. The venue keeps a share of the ticket takings. Agree whether the percentage applies to every ticket or only above a certain number.
  • Ticket with a drink included. Part of the price is a drink or food the bar provides. That part belongs to the venue, and it is worth writing down how much it is worth.

There are other variations (a flat room rental, a guaranteed minimum), but the point is the same: put it in writing. A WhatsApp message with the deal, confirmed by both sides, already saves you the argument at the end of the night. At a minimum it should say:

  • What percentage or amount goes to each side and what it is calculated on.
  • Who sells the tickets and when you settle up (that night, the next day).
  • How many guest spots each side gets.
  • Who provides and pays for sound, lighting, security and the door person.
  • What time soundcheck is, what time doors open and what time you have to finish.

Who sells: only one

The most expensive mistake is the band and the bar selling through different channels with no shared number: the band takes transfers with a list, the bar sells on its account, and on the night of the show there are more people with tickets than room in the venue. One sale, one capacity.

If the band loads the event on a ticketing platform, the band collects and then pays the venue its share according to the deal. If the bar loads it, the other way around. Either way, the other side can follow sales if you share the dashboard numbers, and nobody depends on a spreadsheet made by hand.

The capacity is the venue's, not the one you'd like

Ask the owner for the room's licensed capacity, in writing. Then subtract what the stage, the bar and both sides' guest spots take up. That number is your total capacity, and it is what you load on the ticketing platform so sales close on their own when it fills up. Overselling is not just a comfort problem: it means people who paid and are left outside, and a venue that may have trouble with its license. How to calculate it in detail is in how many tickets to sell based on the venue's licensed capacity.

Price and presale

To set the price, add up what the show costs you (room if there is one, sound, transport, promotion) and divide it by the number of tickets you really think you will sell, not by a full house. If that price doesn't work with what your audience pays, the problem is in the deal or the costs, not in the ticket. Some guidelines that almost always help:

  • A presale cheaper than the door, and said everywhere. If they cost the same, nobody buys early and you reach show day without knowing how many people are coming.
  • A presale with a closing date: "until Thursday" pushes more than "while they last".
  • If the ticket includes a drink, the name should say so, for example "Anticipada + bebida" (advance plus drink). The buyer needs to know what they are paying for.
  • If several bands play, agree among yourselves how it is split. A discount code per band works as a clue: in the dashboard you see how many times each code was used.

The door, on the night of the show

At a bar the door is usually narrow and the line mixes with people stepping out to smoke. Three things keep it in order:

  • One person fixed at the door, who isn't in the band. The musicians are soundchecking or changing right when the line forms.
  • The QR is scanned from a phone. Each QR is valid only once: if someone sent the screenshot to a friend, whoever arrives first gets in. If a ticket is transferred through the official route, the old QR stops working.
  • A guest list closed before doors open. If complimentary tickets are issued from the dashboard, they also go through the scanner and there are no arguments like "I'm on the band's list".

If you are going to keep tickets for the door, see how to sell at the door without holding up the line.

At the end of the night

Settle up with the dashboard numbers in front of you, together with the bar's person. If the deal was a percentage, the math comes from what was sold, not from what someone thinks came in. Then export the buyer base: it is your audience, and it is what helps you announce the next show.

Frequently asked questions

Who should sell the tickets, the band or the bar?

Whatever you agreed, but it is best that only one sells and that it is in writing. If the band loads the event on the ticketing platform, the band collects and then pays the venue its share; if the bar does it, the other way around. What doesn't work is both selling separately without a shared capacity.

How many tickets can I sell for a show at a bar?

Never more than the venue's licensed capacity, minus the space taken by the stage, the bar and guest spots. Ask the owner for the number in writing and load it as the total capacity on the ticketing platform, so sales close on their own when it fills up.

Is it a good idea for the ticket to include a drink?

It can help if the bar asks for it as a condition, but it has to be clear in the ticket name and in the promotion. Agree with the venue who keeps that part of the price and how the drink is handed out, for example with a stamp or a wristband when the QR is scanned.

Do I receive 100% of the ticket price if I sell with ENTRÁ?

Yes. You receive 100% of the price you set for each ticket, and service and processing fees are paid by the buyer, with the breakdown visible before paying. Whatever is owed to the bar you settle with the venue yourself, according to the deal.
