Sponsors for a small event: what to offer and how to ask

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 7 min read

A two hundred person event is of no interest to a big brand, but it is to the bar on the corner, the neighborhood brewery, the music school or the shop that sells what your audience likes. With them you almost always end up with a trade, sometimes some cash. To get it you need to know what you are offering, show numbers you can back up and ask for it on a single page.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

First, what you are missing

Before writing to anyone, list what the event costs you and mark the things someone else could provide. Looking for "a sponsor" is not the same as looking for someone to supply the bar drinks, the van for the gear or the printing of the wristbands. With the list in hand:

  • You know who to write to. If you need drinks, you go to a distributor or a brewery; if you need sound, to a rental company that wants to show off its gear.
  • You know what your ask is worth. If sound costs you a specific amount, that is the value of the trade, and you can offer something proportionate in return.
  • You don't accept things you can't use. A hundred branded T-shirts don't lower any cost if you weren't going to give away T-shirts.

If you haven't closed the event's numbers yet, start there: how many tickets to sell so you don't lose money sorts out your costs and break-even point before you go out and ask.

What you can offer

A small event has three things that can be useful to a local brand: being seen, being named and reaching an audience it cares about. Offer concrete things, that you can deliver and later show.

  • Presence at the venue. A banner on the stage or the bar, a stand or a table to show its product, the brand on the wristband or the drink token. Say where it goes and how big.
  • Mentions. In the promotional posts, in the event description, on stage before the main act. State how many: "three posts before the event and one live mention" can be checked; "social media promotion" can't.
  • Product in hand. Its drink is the one at the bar, its food is the one at the food stand, it hands out a sample at the door.
  • Its own activation. A raffle, a discount for attendees, a tasting. The brand sets it up and you give it the space and the moment.

Audience data: with consent or not at all

Many brands will ask you for "the list". Don't give it to them. Whoever bought a ticket left you their name, email and ID number (DNI) to get into your event, not to receive a third party's advertising. Handing that list to a sponsor can get you into trouble and, above all, burns you with your own audience.

What you can offer:

  • Aggregate numbers: how many tickets sold, how many people came in, which channels the sales came from. Nothing that identifies anyone.
  • Let the brand collect its own contacts at the event, with a QR code to its form or a raffle box, where each person decides whether to leave their details and knows what for.
  • An optional question at checkout, if the brand insists on that route. In ENTRÁ you can add your own questions to the event, which appear at purchase, can be optional and show up in the spreadsheet. A clear, optional question such as "Do you want [brand] to email you its promo? Yes / No", with the brand's name in plain sight, leaves the decision to the buyer. If you use it, pass on only those who said yes, and nothing beyond what they agreed to.

Real numbers from the dashboard

What convinces a small brand most is not a big number but a believable one. If you have already opened sales, you have data you can show exactly as it comes out of the ticketing dashboard:

  • Tickets sold, by type. How many general admission, how many early bird, how many tables. If you already ran another date, that total too.
  • Where sales came from. The ENTRÁ dashboard has a block with that name that groups purchases by source and, if you used utm links, by campaign. There you can see how much Instagram sold, how much a WhatsApp group, how much the link from the bar where the band plays.
  • The export. The buyer spreadsheet includes the source and campaign columns. Remove the personal data and keep the totals: purchases by channel, by day, by ticket type.
  • How many people came in. After the event, the scanner tells you how many QR codes were used. That is real attendance, not "interested".

One move is very convincing: build the brand its own utm link (for example, with the brand's name as the campaign) for it to post on its social media. Afterwards you show how many tickets sold through that link. It is the simplest way for it to see that its audience and yours overlap. How to build those links is in how to promote an event and track what sells, and what to look at in the dashboard in the sales data your ticketing platform gives you.

Don't show market figures or reach promises you can't prove. If it is your first edition and there is no history, say so, and offer to send the report after the event.

Trade or cash

For a small event, a trade is the easiest thing to get and often the most useful. A couple of criteria:

  • A trade is worth what it saves you. If the brewery gives you the kegs you were going to buy, that is money. If it gives you something you weren't going to use, it isn't.
  • Put a value on it in writing. Even with no money changing hands, write down what each side contributes. That way you know whether your offer is proportionate and you don't give away the stage for a box of cups.
  • Cash, when the brand wants something that costs you. If it asks for exclusivity at the bar, a big stand or its name in the event title, that has a price.

If the brand puts in money, think about how you will collect it and invoice it before closing the deal. If you are registered under Argentina's simplified small taxpayer regime (monotributo), invoicing ticket sales under the monotributo gives you the general picture; for your specific case, ask your accountant.

The one page proposal

Nobody reads a twenty page deck for a two hundred person event. One page is enough, and it needs to have this:

  • What the event is, in two lines: what happens, where, when, for whom.
  • Who comes: the type of audience, approximate age, where they come from. No inflating.
  • The numbers you have: tickets sold for other dates or this one, real attendance, which channels people came from. All with the source (the dashboard, the scanner).
  • What you offer, as a list: presence, mentions, product, activation. With quantities.
  • What you ask for: the specific trade or the amount, and when you need it.
  • What you send afterwards: photos where its brand is visible, the published mentions and the event's numbers.

Send it to whoever makes the decision, not the community manager. If the business is small, going in person with the printed page works better.

After the event

What you do afterwards decides whether the brand comes back. Within the week, send a short report: photos with its brand in view, screenshots of the posts where you named it, how many people came in and, if you built its link, how many tickets came from it. If something didn't go as promised, say so. A brand that sees you deliver, even if the event was small, is the one that says yes next time.

Frequently asked questions

Can I give the sponsor the list of buyers?

No. Whoever bought a ticket gave you their details to get into your event, not for you to hand them to a brand. Show the sponsor aggregate numbers: how many tickets, which channels they came from, how many people came in. If the brand wants contacts, it should collect them at the event with its own form, where each person chooses whether to leave them.

Is a trade or cash better?

It depends on what you are missing. A trade works if it replaces something you were going to pay for anyway, like drinks, sound or printing. If it is something you don't need, it doesn't lower any cost. Cash gives you more freedom, but it is harder to get for a first edition.

What numbers do I show a brand if it is my first event?

Whatever you have that is yours: real followers, inquiries, how many tickets you have sold if sales are already open and which channel they came from. If there is no history, offer a small deal and commit to sending the report afterwards, with what really happened.
