How many tickets you need to sell so you don't lose money: your event's break-even point

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

Before opening sales there is only one question that matters: how many tickets do I have to sell so the event doesn't cost me money? That number is the break-even point. It is not hard to calculate, but it is almost always calculated wrong because people forget the costs that grow with each person, the complimentary tickets and the cushion. Here is the full calculation, step by step, with an example.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

The four pieces of the calculation

To know how many tickets you need, you have to have four numbers written down, not in your head:

  • Fixed costs. What you pay whether ten people show up or five hundred: venue rental, sound and lights, artists, security hired per night, insurance, design and promotion, door staff.
  • Cost per person. What grows with every person who comes in: an included drink, the wristband or the cup, extra cleaning or bar staff calculated by audience size.
  • Net price. What actually reaches your pocket for each ticket, not what the audience pays.
  • What each ticket leaves you. Net price minus cost per person.

There is only one formula: fixed costs divided by what each ticket leaves you. The result, rounded up, is the number of paid tickets you need to sell to break even.

The net price: which number to use

The most common mistake is doing the math with the price the audience sees and finding out later that the ticketing platform took its cut from you.

With ENTRÁ the organizer keeps 100% of the price they set. The service and payment processing costs are paid by the buyer, separately and in plain sight. So the net price is the same price you loaded in the dashboard. If you sell with another tool, ask exactly how much they deduct from you per ticket and subtract it before going on.

A step by step example

All the numbers in this example are hypothetical and rounded, chosen so the math is easy to follow. They are not market prices or a reference for what an event costs: replace them with yours.

Say you have a party at a venue with a capacity of 500 people:

  • Fixed costs: ARS 3,000,000.
  • Cost per person: ARS 2,000.
  • Net ticket price: ARS 10,000.

Each ticket leaves you ARS 10,000 minus ARS 2,000, that is ARS 8,000. Divide ARS 3,000,000 by ARS 8,000 and you get 375 tickets. That is your break-even point: with 375 paid tickets, you are even. From ticket 376 on, you make money.

Look at what happens if you forget the cost per person and divide straight by ARS 10,000: you get 300. You would be 75 tickets short of covering costs and you would find out after the event.

When tiers have different prices

If you sell a cheaper early bird tier, the math changes, because those tickets leave you less. Following the same hypothetical example, say the first 150 tickets go for ARS 8,000 net:

  • Each early bird ticket leaves you ARS 8,000 minus ARS 2,000: ARS 6,000. The 150 bring in ARS 900,000.
  • That leaves ARS 2,100,000 to cover with general admission, which leaves you ARS 8,000 each: 263 tickets (262.5 rounded up).
  • In total you need 413 tickets, not 375.

The early bird brings money in early and creates urgency, but it raises the number of tickets you need. Do the math with your real tiers.

Complimentary tickets count too

A complimentary ticket brings no income, but the person who comes in with it does bring the cost per person and takes up a spot. If in the example you give out 40 complimentary tickets, that is 40 times ARS 2,000: ARS 80,000 more in costs. Divided by the ARS 8,000 each general ticket leaves you, that is 10 more paid tickets to cover them.

On top of that, those 40 people take up space in a 500 person venue. If your break-even is already high, every complimentary ticket shrinks your room to sell. How many to give and to whom is covered in complimentary tickets and invitations: how many to give.

The cushion: the number that protects you

No event turns out exactly like the spreadsheet: an expense you didn't have shows up, or a refund. That is why it pays to add a cushion for the unexpected to your fixed costs before dividing.

In the example, if you set aside ARS 300,000 as a cushion, that is 38 more tickets (37.5 rounded up). Adding it all up (tiers, complimentary tickets and cushion), the number you need to aim for ends up at 461 tickets in a 500 person venue.

And that is a warning sign. When break-even is that close to capacity, the event is fragile: a rainy night or a clashing date leaves you at a loss. The ways out are the usual ones: cut fixed costs, review the price or choose a better sized venue. And the capacity that counts is the licensed one, not what you think fits: see how many tickets to sell based on the venue's licensed capacity.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate how many tickets I have to sell so I don't lose money?

Subtract from the net ticket price what each person who comes in costs you: that is what each ticket leaves you. Then divide the event's total fixed costs by that number. The result, rounded up, is the number of paid tickets you need to cover your expenses.

Do complimentary tickets change the break-even point?

Yes. A complimentary ticket brings no income but it does bring the cost per person (included drink, wristband, cleaning) and takes up a spot you could have sold. Add the cost of all complimentary tickets to your fixed costs before dividing and you will see how many extra paid tickets you need to cover them.

What price do I use for the math if in ENTRÁ the buyer pays a service charge?

Use the price you set. With ENTRÁ the organizer keeps 100% of that price and the service costs are paid by the buyer separately, in plain sight. With other ticketing platforms, before doing the math, subtract what they deduct from you for each ticket.

What happens if the break-even point is very close to the venue's capacity?

The event becomes fragile: any rain, a clashing date or an expense you didn't foresee leaves you at a loss. If you need to sell almost the whole venue to cover costs, it is better to cut fixed costs, change venues or review the price before opening sales.
