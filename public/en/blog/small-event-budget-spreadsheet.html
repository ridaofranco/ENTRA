Budget for a small event: the spreadsheet before you sell

By SOMOS DER · October 29, 2026 · 7 min read

Many small events lose money for a simple reason: sales opened before anyone knew what the night cost. The price came from what the venue next door charged, the sound system showed up at the last minute and the math was done the following Monday. A one sheet spreadsheet, built before publishing, tells you how much you will spend, how many tickets you need to cover it and how much margin you have if something goes wrong. This guide goes in that order.

A note before you start: ENTRÁ is an Argentine ticketing platform. It sells tickets for events held in Argentina, priced in Argentine pesos (ARS). What this guide says applies to most events; when it describes how ENTRÁ works, it means in Argentina.

Why build it before going on sale

Once the ticket is published, sharply lowering or raising the price is noticeable and confuses people who already bought. With the budget done beforehand you know three things you need to load the event:

  • The minimum price below which selling isn't worth it.
  • How many tickets you need to sell so you don't lose money.
  • When you need the money, which tells you whether a presale makes sense to cover deposits.

If it is your first time organizing, the full list of decisions to make beforehand is in the checklist for organizing your first event.

The spreadsheet: which columns to have

You don't need a system. A spreadsheet with these columns is enough:

  • Item: what it is (venue, sound, DJ, flyer).
  • Type: fixed or variable.
  • Amount: for variable items, the amount per person.
  • Status: confirmed (there is a written quote) or estimated (you worked it out yourself).
  • When it is paid: deposit, before the event, on the night or afterwards.
  • Who pays it: you, a partner, the venue or a sponsor.

The status column is the most important. A budget with half its rows "estimated" is a bet, and you want to know that before setting the price.

Fixed costs

These are the ones you pay whether ten people come or the place fills up. At a small event they are usually most of the spending:

  • Venue: rent, or the minimum spend the bar asks for if you work on a bar deal. Ask what it includes: sound, bar staff, cleaning, security.
  • Artists: a fixed fee, if there is one. If the deal is a percentage of sales, it goes as variable.
  • Production: sound, lights, operator, stage, screen.
  • Staff: door, security, production.
  • Promotion and design: flyer, social media ads, printing.
  • Paperwork: permits, music rights (in Argentina, SADAIC), insurance, depending on the venue and type of event. Ask about them beforehand; if you don't know, load them as estimated.
  • Team transport and food: the ones that always get forgotten.

Variable costs

They grow with each person who comes in. At many small events there are few of them, but if you forget them your break even math comes out wrong:

  • A drink included in the ticket, if there is one: what each drink costs you, not what the bar charges.
  • Food per person, if the ticket includes it.
  • Wristbands, stamps or welcome gifts.
  • A percentage for artists or for the venue, if the deal is based on sales.

Complimentary tickets count too: they bring in no income, but they take up space and, if the ticket includes a drink, they create variable cost. How many to give without them eating up the night is in complimentary tickets and invitations: how many to give.

The service charge doesn't go in your spreadsheet

With ENTRÁ, the price you set for each ticket is what you receive: 100%. On top of that price, the buyer pays a service charge that covers ENTRÁ's service and payment processing, and sees it itemized at checkout before confirming. The total shown there is what is charged; nothing is added afterwards. For free events, reservations carry no charge.

For your budget that means income per ticket is the price you enter, with no fee subtracted. What you do need to check with your accountant are the taxes that apply to what you collect.

How many tickets to break even

With fixed and variable costs separated, there is just one calculation:

Tickets to break even = fixed costs ÷ (ticket price − variable cost per person)

A case with example numbers, only to show the math, not as a price reference:

  • Fixed costs: ARS 1,200,000.
  • Ticket price: ARS 10,000.
  • Variable cost per person (one drink included): ARS 2,000.
  • What each ticket leaves you: ARS 10,000 − ARS 2,000 = ARS 8,000.
  • Tickets to break even: ARS 1,200,000 ÷ ARS 8,000 = 150 tickets.

If the venue is licensed for 200 people, you need to sell 75% to break even. The spreadsheet is already telling you something: the margin is thin, and you should cut fixed costs, raise the price or look for a trade deal. For the full step by step, with price tiers and complimentary tickets, see how many tickets to sell so you don't lose money.

If you have several tiers at different prices, do the math with a realistic average price: if you expect to sell half in presale, the average ends up closer to the presale price than the door price.

Surprises: a line of their own

Something always happens: a cable you have to buy, a delivery that costs more, an extra hour at the venue. Instead of hiding a cushion in every row, add a separate line called "contingency", calculated as a percentage of fixed costs. 10% is a reasonable starting point; if you have many estimated amounts or it is your first time at that venue, leave more.

Following the example numbers: 10% of ARS 1,200,000 is ARS 120,000. Fixed costs rise to ARS 1,320,000 and the tickets to break even, to 165. That difference of 15 tickets is what protects you from a surprise.

Three scenarios before publishing

With the spreadsheet built, add three result rows at the bottom:

  • Weak: you sell half of what you expect. How much do you lose and can you absorb it?
  • Expected: what you really think you will sell, not a full house.
  • Full: the whole quota sold. It is the ceiling, not the plan.

If the weak scenario leaves you with a loss you can't pay, you still have time to change something before loading the event: a smaller venue, less production, a sponsor who covers part of it. For that last one, see how to find sponsors for a small event.

During sales, the ENTRÁ dashboard shows tickets sold, revenue and the breakdown by ticket type in real time. Compare those numbers with your spreadsheet every few days: if halfway through you are below the weak scenario, that is the time to step up promotion, not the night before.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a fixed cost and a variable cost at an event?

A fixed cost is paid whether ten or two hundred people come: venue rent, sound, the agreed fee, promotion. A variable cost grows with each person who comes in: an included drink, a wristband, a portion of food. Separating them is what lets you calculate how many tickets you need to sell.

Do I have to add the ticketing platform's fee to my costs?

With ENTRÁ, no. You receive 100% of the price you set for each ticket. The service charge, which covers ENTRÁ's service and payment processing, is paid by the buyer, who sees it itemized at checkout before confirming. With other platforms, check whether the fee comes out of your price.

How much should I leave for contingencies?

A line of its own in the spreadsheet, calculated as a percentage of fixed costs, for example 10%. If you have many unconfirmed costs or it is your first event at that venue, leave more. What isn't used is profit; what is missing comes out of your pocket.
